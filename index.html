Why

I don't want to do something this hard for no reason.

I'm Blake. I'm 26, I live in Raleigh, and I work as an automation engineer on a pharmaceutical manufacturing site in Holly Springs.

My job is process control — keeping a system inside its limits while it's under load. Endurance sport turned out to be the same problem with worse instrumentation. You manage fuel, heat, and failure modes for hours on end and try to keep any one variable from running away from you. I've done Ironman racing for a few years now. A hundred miles on foot is the next thing I don't know if I can do.

But I've never been able to do something like this just to have done it. Sitting at mile 70 at three in the morning, "I wanted to see if I could" is not a good enough answer. I needed a better one, and it turned out to be the thing that had been staring at me the whole time: the choice itself.

I get to choose my hard thing. I train for it, I schedule it, and when it's over, it stops. Millions of people wake up inside a hard thing they never chose — and theirs doesn't stop.

Cancer. Hunger. Abuse. Neglect. Disease. Loss. Nobody signs up for any of it. There's no training block, no aid station, no cutoff time. The only honest advantage I have is that my suffering is optional — so Both Clocks exists to spend that advantage on people whose suffering isn't. Every event this project takes on will sponsor a charity that serves them: money raised goes directly to that cause, and I put my body through something worth watching so more people look.

The model

One event. One cause. Every dollar direct.

Both Clocks holds no money. Each event sponsors one charity, announced before the event, and every donation goes straight to that charity through its own donation page.

How I pick a cause

Four criteria, checked in this order:

Serving the unchosen. Cancer, hunger, abuse, neglect, disease, loss — the cause serves people living through something nobody signs up for.

Small enough that the money moves the needle. A five-figure gift to a national organization disappears. To a local one, it's a visible fraction of a year.

Direct service, not regranting. The charity does the work itself. There's no layer between the donors and the person helped.

A clean unit of impact. You should be able to say exactly what a gift bought, which matters when you're asking people for money for months on end.

What a hundred miles actually involves

If you've never watched one of these, the honest description is: it's a very long day of eating and walking, punctuated by running, with a few hours in the middle of the night where the whole thing becomes a negotiation with yourself.

The course is a loop, so I'll come through the start/finish area repeatedly. That's where my crew sits — resupplying food, swapping shoes and socks, changing headlamp batteries, and telling me I look fine when I don't.

The failure modes aren't usually the legs. They're the stomach shutting down, the temperature drop after sunset, and the low point around 2–4am when body temperature bottoms out and quitting starts making a lot of sense. Getting through that stretch is most of what the training is for.

The festival part

Southern Tour is deliberately not a solemn event — it's been running since 2013 as a running festival for the region, with camping, a bonfire, live music, and a well-earned reputation for beer. There are shorter events on the same weekend: a 50-mile, a 50K, and a 50-mile team relay.
