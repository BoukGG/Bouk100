Why

I choose to suffer. That makes me one of the lucky ones.

I'm Blake. I'm 26, I live in Raleigh, and I work as an automation engineer on a pharmaceutical manufacturing site in Holly Springs. Until a year ago, I had never pushed past a real limit in my life.

That changed in the fall of 2025, when I started endurance training. A half marathon in Wilmington that February. A full marathon in Nashville that April. I trained seriously, beat my goal time, and collected the compliments — people telling me I was a beast, that they could never. It felt good. And underneath it, something was missing: I couldn't answer the simplest question about the whole thing. Why are you doing this? A medal wasn't a reason. "To see if I could" wasn't a reason. I knew I couldn't stop at a marathon, and I knew the next distance wouldn't be the answer either.

In July I ran 40 miles in a day — eight hours through Carolina heat. Somewhere deep in that run, legs gone, the voice in my head making its case for quitting over and over, I found the two things I never expected to find there: more gratitude than I've felt anywhere else in my life, and the closest I have ever felt to God. I learned that the desire to quit is never everlasting — it passes, if you give its energy somewhere else to go. And I finally saw my suffering for what it actually is: a privilege. I scheduled it. I paid an entry fee for it. I could stop at any time.

I get to choose my hard thing. I train for it, I schedule it, and when it's over, it stops. Millions of people wake up inside a hard thing they never chose — and theirs doesn't stop.

Cancer. Hunger. Abuse. Neglect. Disease. Loss. Nobody signs up for any of it — no training block, no aid station, no cutoff time. And nothing guarantees me the health or the luck to keep doing what I do: both clocks are always ticking, mine included. So that's what Both Clocks is — me spending my luck while I still have it. Every event this project takes on sponsors a charity serving people inside something they never chose, and every dollar raised goes directly to them.

Here's the honest part. I've been running for barely a year. I don't know if I can run 100 miles. I don't know if this campaign will raise a single dollar. I'm putting the same effort into both anyway — every training mile logged in public, every dollar routed straight to the cause — and I'm letting you watch whether it works or not.

I get to quit. Most people never get that option. That's why I won't.

What scares me isn't mile 70 at three in the morning. It's reaching the end of a comfortable life knowing I never tried hard enough to matter to the people around me. Both Clocks is how I make sure that doesn't happen.

If reading this stirs something — maybe a little guilt about your own comfort zone — don't sit in that feeling. Trade it for motivation. You already suffer for what you have; work takes its hours out of everyone. If my suffering moves you, spend a little of what yours has earned on people whose suffering was never optional. Or share this story with one person. Or go do your own hard thing, and tell me about it.

The model

One event. One cause. Every dollar direct.

Both Clocks holds no money. Each event sponsors one charity, announced before the event, and every donation goes straight to that charity through its own donation page.

01

An event gets chosen

Something long, hard, and worth watching — an ultra, a climb, whatever earns attention honestly. First up: 100 miles in 24 hours at the Southern Tour Ultra.

02

A cause gets sponsored

One charity per event, serving people in the middle of something they never chose. Announced before the event, with the reasons why published right here.

03

The money goes direct

Donations go to the charity's own page — tax receipts included, my hands never touch it. Shop profits from the event's merch go to the same cause.

How I pick a cause

Four criteria, checked in this order:

Serving the unchosen. Cancer, hunger, abuse, neglect, disease, loss — the cause serves people living through something nobody signs up for.

Small enough that the money moves the needle. A five-figure gift to a national organization disappears. To a local one, it's a visible fraction of a year.

Direct service, not regranting. The charity does the work itself. There's no layer between the donors and the person helped.

A clean unit of impact. You should be able to say exactly what a gift bought, which matters when you're asking people for money for months on end.

What a hundred miles actually involves

If you've never watched one of these, the honest description is: it's a very long day of eating and walking, punctuated by running, with a few hours in the middle of the night where the whole thing becomes a negotiation with yourself.

The course is a loop, so I'll come through the start/finish area repeatedly. That's where my crew sits — resupplying food, swapping shoes and socks, changing headlamp batteries, and telling me I look fine when I don't.

The failure modes aren't usually the legs. They're the stomach shutting down, the temperature drop after sunset, and the low point around 2–4am when body temperature bottoms out and quitting starts making a lot of sense. Getting through that stretch is most of what the training is for.

The festival part

Southern Tour is deliberately not a solemn event — it's been running since 2013 as a running festival for the region, with camping, a bonfire, live music, and a well-earned reputation for beer. There are shorter events on the same weekend: a 50-mile, a 50K, and a 50-mile team relay.
