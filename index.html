Why

I don't want to do something this hard for no reason.

I'm Blake. I'm 26, I live in Raleigh, and I work as an automation engineer on a pharmaceutical manufacturing site in Holly Springs.

My job is process control — keeping a system inside its limits while it's under load. Endurance sport turned out to be the same problem with worse instrumentation. You manage fuel, heat, and failure modes for hours on end and try to keep any one variable from running away from you. I've done Ironman racing for a few years now. A hundred miles on foot is the next thing I don't know if I can do.

But I've never been able to do something like this just to have done it. Sitting at mile 70 at three in the morning, "I wanted to see if I could" is not a good enough answer. I needed the day to be worth something to someone other than me.

I get to choose to be hungry and exhausted for a day, and then it stops. That's the whole difference, and it's the reason I picked this cause.

The race runs through New Hanover County. So does the problem. Kids there eat breakfast and lunch at school five days a week, and then Friday afternoon comes and the food stops until Monday. That's roughly 67 hours — almost three times as long as my race — and unlike my race, nobody signed up for it and there's no finish line at the end of it.

Where the money goes

NourishNC feeds hungry kids in New Hanover County.

A Wilmington-based 501(c)(3). Local, small, and direct — the money buys food rather than passing through a granting layer on the way.

Three programs

What they actually do

01

The Backpack Program

Kids from pre-K through 5th grade go home with three meals a day for every weekend and every school break. This is the program that closes the 67-hour gap.

02

The Market on Market

A full grocery store where food-insecure kids and their families shop and choose their own food, rather than receiving a prepacked box. Dignity is part of the design.

03

Mobile MoM

A custom-built mobile version of the market that goes to neighborhoods where getting to a store is the barrier.

Why I chose them

I had four criteria, and I checked them in this order:

Local to the race. Wilmington and New Hanover County, not a national chapter with a local office.

Small enough that the money moves the needle. A five-figure gift to a national organization disappears. Here it's a visible fraction of a year.

Direct service, not regranting. They buy and distribute the food themselves. There's no layer between my donors and a kid eating.

A clean unit of impact. Food is measurable. You can say exactly what a gift bought, which matters when you're asking people for money for six straight months.

Last year they provided over a million meals.

What a hundred miles actually involves

If you've never watched one of these, the honest description is: it's a very long day of eating and walking, punctuated by running, with a few hours in the middle of the night where the whole thing becomes a negotiation with yourself.

The course is a loop, so I'll come through the start/finish area repeatedly. That's where my crew sits — resupplying food, swapping shoes and socks, changing headlamp batteries, and telling me I look fine when I don't.

The failure modes aren't usually the legs. They're the stomach shutting down, the temperature drop after sunset, and the low point around 2–4am when body temperature bottoms out and quitting starts making a lot of sense. Getting through that stretch is most of what the training is for.

The festival part

Southern Tour is deliberately not a solemn event — it's been running since 2013 as a running festival for the region, with camping, a bonfire, live music, and a well-earned reputation for beer. There are shorter events on the same weekend: a 50-mile, a 50K, and a 50-mile team relay.

If you want to come: you'd be very welcome, and having people at the loop at 3am is worth more than I can explain. [Add your crew contact or a link to race registration if you want people to join a relay team.]
